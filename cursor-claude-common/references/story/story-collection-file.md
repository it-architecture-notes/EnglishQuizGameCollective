But overnight, the mountain shook, and a massive, ancient dragon named Emberclaw awakened, covering the valley in thick ash and blocking the village's vital water supply with a mountain of boulders. The villagers are terrified and ready to abandon their homes, but a young, determined girl named Lily refuses to give up. Arming herself with nothing but her father's old leather satchel and a notebook of local legends, Lily sets off up the mountain to find a way to soothe the beast and clear the water path. She knows she can't do it alone—she will need the cleverness, strength, and cooperation of friends she meets along the way to save Oakhaven.
The story progresses through:

The Library That Grows — Illustration Guide
Story Overview
Maple Grove School was once a quiet, easy place to find a good book. But when a nearby school closed for major repairs, dozens of new students arrived almost overnight, and the library could no longer keep up. Lines grew long, favorite books went missing or fell apart from overuse, and the librarian did her best with too little to work with. Four friends—Ivy, Dash, Priya, and Mustafa—decide they won't just wait for someone else to fix it. Armed with cardboard boxes, sticky labels, and a lot of determination, they set out to rebuild the library shelf by shelf, book by book, for every student who walks through its doors.
The story progresses through: Recognizing the Problem → Gathering the Books → Judging What Belongs → Restoring Order → Building a Lasting System → Welcoming Every Reader
The emotional progression: Concern → Energy → Focus → Satisfaction → Ingenuity → Warm Belonging
Each illustration should communicate kindness, cooperation, and the quiet power of a few kids choosing to help.
Illustration Philosophy
Each image should feel like a page from a warm children's storybook.
The illustration should naturally guide the viewer's attention:
First toward the main problem or task at hand (crowded shelves, unlabeled boxes, a cramped reading corner).
Then toward the characters actively working or helping.
Then toward the emotional expressions of teamwork and care.
Finally toward small environmental details that enrich the story.
Each slide will be paired with a short caption below the image (not rendered inside the illustration itself). Illustrations should leave calm, uncluttered space near the bottom third of the composition for this caption, without designing text or UI into the artwork itself.

Line style: dark brown or charcoal outlines (never pure black).

Movement: Show motion only through small environmental movement, drifting dust motes, fluttering pages, or soft motion lines. Avoid exaggerated cartoon poses or action-comic effects.

The Bridge of Wobbling Wood — Illustration Guide
Story Overview
The village of Willowbrook had a problem: the only bridge connecting the village to the school, the market, and the doctor's office was old and wobbly, and only children were light enough to cross it safely. Ben was the oldest child in the village, but he had a secret — he was terribly afraid of heights. One morning, his little sister Mia crossed the bridge alone to prove she was brave, and a plank broke beneath her foot, leaving her stranded and scared halfway across. Ben's heart raced and his hands trembled, but he loved Mia more than he feared the bridge — and with a reader's help at every step, he'll have to find the courage to cross and bring her home.
The story progresses through: Finding Courage → Looking Carefully → Staying Calm → Thinking Like a Helper → Working Together → Safe on Solid Ground
The emotional progression: Fear → Determination → Focus → Calm → Cleverness → Trust → Relief and Pride
Each illustration should communicate that bravery isn't the absence of fear — it's taking the next step anyway, with a little help from others.
Illustration Philosophy
Each image should feel like a page from a warm, gently suspenseful children's storybook.
The illustration should naturally guide the viewer's attention:
First toward the main problem or obstacle at hand (a shaking bridge, a cracked plank, a stuck foot).
Then toward Ben and Mia actively responding to it.
Then toward the emotional expressions of fear turning into courage.
Finally toward small environmental details that enrich the story.
Each slide will be paired with a short caption below the image (not rendered inside the illustration itself) — drawn from the story's "Reader Action" lines. Reserve approximately the lower 20–25% of every illustration as visually calm space (grass, bridge deck, mist, or sky with minimal detail) so captions remain readable without covering important artwork.
Universal Illustration Rules
Camera distance: Keep a consistent medium-wide framing across every scene — far enough to show the bridge and valley context, close enough to read Ben and Mia's expressions clearly.
One focal point per page: Every illustration should have a single clear visual anchor (the first step onto the bridge, the mossy vs. roped plank, the swaying ropes, the stuck foot, the joined hands, solid ground). Supporting details should stay secondary and never compete with it.
Limit scene complexity: No more than three distinct visual groupings per illustration (for example: Ben and Mia, the bridge hazard, and the valley background). Avoid cluttering a single scene with too many competing activities.
Character scale: Ben and Mia should always appear correctly proportioned to the bridge and valley — small enough that the crossing feels like a real challenge, but never so small that their expressions become unreadable.
Tension, not terror: This story carries more suspense than the others in this series, and that's appropriate — but the art must always communicate "this is difficult" rather than "someone is in mortal danger." See the Avoid/Negative Prompt section below.
Environment Reference Sheet
Willowbrook Valley Environment
Willowbrook is a cozy village split by a river valley, connected only by a single old rope-and-plank bridge. The setting remains visually consistent throughout the story.

A long wooden plank bridge with rope handrails, strung between two grassy cliff edges
Weathered grey-brown wooden planks, some newer, most old and worn
Thick knotted rope railings on either side, slightly frayed in places
Gaps and missing planks increasing in number as the story progresses
Patches of green moss on some boards near the village side

A shallow but fast-moving river running below the bridge, visible but not violent
Grassy, gently sloped cliffs on both sides (not sheer or jagged)
Wildflowers and low shrubs growing near the bridge's anchor points
Soft morning mist in the valley early in the story, clearing by the end
The Village
Cozy thatched-roof cottages with round windows on the near side
A small schoolhouse, a market stall row, and a doctor's office visible on the far side
A dirt path leading up to the bridge from the village side
A small gathered crowd of villagers near the story's end
Every illustration should clearly feel like the same bridge and valley, viewed from different points along the crossing rather than a completely different setting.

Title/Setup Slide: perched near the bridge's entrance post, watching.
Challenge 1: perched on the entrance rope beside Ben.
Challenge 2: flown a little further ahead, perched near the safe plank.
Challenge 3: waits on the rope railing during the windy gust.

Art Style
Soft children's-storybook watercolor illustration.
Style characteristics: loose hand-painted ink linework, visible brush texture, muted pastel colors, warm cream tones, weathered wood browns, soft misty valley greens and blues.
Characters: rounded simplified faces, small dot eyes, minimal facial features, soft body proportions, warm pink cheek blush.
Line style: dark brown or charcoal outlines (never pure black).
Backgrounds: loosely painted, atmospheric, impressionistic, fading softly toward edges with white paper negative space around the frame (not a full-bleed digital background).
Movement: Show motion only through small environmental movement — a swaying rope, drifting mist, a fluttering leaf, gentle ripples on the river. Avoid exaggerated cartoon poses or action-comic effects, even during the windier, more tense moments.
Color Palette
Maintain a consistent storybook palette throughout the book.
Primary colors:
Warm cream
Weathered wood brown
Misty sage green
Soft river blue
Muted moss green
Gentle overcast grey
Warm golden light (introduced gradually as the story resolves)
Accent colors:
Soft pink cheeks
Mia's red ribbons
Pale morning-mist white
Avoid highly saturated colors or strong color shifts between illustrations, except for the intentional shift from cool, misty tones to warm golden light as the crossing succeeds.
Avoid / Negative Prompt
This story carries more suspense than the others in this series, and that tension is intentional — but it must always read as "this is difficult," never as "someone is in danger of dying."
Do not include:
speech bubbles
comic panels
game UI
interface buttons
photorealism
3D rendering
anime or manga style
heavy black outlines
neon colors
exaggerated cartoon proportions
a visible fall, a character losing their footing completely, or any character shown falling toward the river
a steep, jagged, or vertigo-inducing drop — the valley should read as a real but survivable height
panicked or terrified facial expressions (wide screaming mouths, tears of terror) — use furrowed brows, gripped ropes, and stiff posture instead
visible injury, blood, or peril to any character
a raging, violent, or turbulent river — keep the water fast-moving but clearly shallow and non-threatening
visual chaos
Show the difficulty through wobbling boards, taut ropes, worried (not terrified) expressions, and teamwork — not through implied danger to life. Written text should never become the focus of an illustration.
Character Reference Sheet
Ben: 11 years old, tousled sandy-brown hair, deep green sweater with rolled-up sleeves, brown trousers, worn boots. Personality: Caring, quietly courageous despite a genuine fear of heights — his bravery is shown through action even while his body language shows fear.
Mia: 7 years old, hair in two braids tied with red ribbons, yellow dress, small brown boots. Personality: Adventurous, eager to prove herself, scared but trusting once Ben reaches her.
Grandpa: referenced only in narration and dialogue — never illustrated. Ben remembers Grandpa's words, but this should be shown purely through Ben's own posture and expression (a pause, a deep breath, a steadying grip on the rope), not through any painted memory image. Keeping the visual world limited to characters who are actually present keeps the illustrations simple and consistent.
Villagers: a small group of warm, ordinary townsfolk (a mix of ages) who gather at the bridge's edge only in the Final Scene, visibly relieved and proud rather than having taken any active role in the rescue.
Character Consistency Rules Ben and Mia's height difference, hairstyles, clothing colors, facial proportions, and overall appearance should remain identical throughout every illustration. Their clothing may become slightly windblown, dusty, or damp with mist as the crossing continues, but no character should unexpectedly change hairstyle, clothing color, accessories, or apparent age. Ben should never appear physically larger or older than his established design, even in wider shots.
Global Continuity Rules
Maintain identical character appearance, clothing, and the layout of the bridge and valley across all scenes. The journey moves along a single continuous crossing: the village-side bridge entrance → partway across near the mossy and roped planks → the swaying midpoint → Mia's stuck position further along → the final stretch to the far cliff edge → solid ground on the far side. The bridge should show cumulative, consistent damage (the same missing planks and cracks reappearing in later scenes) rather than resetting between illustrations.
The original broken plank — the one that snapped beneath Mia's foot in the Title/Setup Slide — should remain the story's fixed landmark. It stays visible, unchanged, in the middle distance or background of every later scene (Challenges 2 through 5 and the Final Scene), letting readers visually track how far Ben and Mia have traveled past it. New hazards (the mossy plank, the falling board in Challenge 5) are additional and distinct from this original landmark, not replacements for it.
Physical wear progression: Ben and Mia should look gradually more "well-traveled" as the crossing continues, without ever looking distressed or unkempt:
Ben's sweater sleeves become rolled up and slightly dusty by Challenge 3 onward.

Isabella & the Gray World — Illustration Guide
Story Overview
Every flower and animal in the forest has turned gray. The sky itself looks tired, like a painting left out in the rain. Someone is taking the world's colors, one by one, and no one knows why. A curious, kind-hearted girl named Isabella sets out to follow the clues and uncover the mystery before every color disappears forever — and she'll need a reader's help at every step to solve it.
The story progresses through: The First Clue → Choosing the Right Trail → Earning Trust → Reaching the Hidden Cave → Understanding the Truth → Color Restored
Isabella's emotional arc: Curiosity and Hope → Concentration → Growing Empathy → Nervous Determination → Compassion → Joy and Pride
Each illustration should communicate curiosity, gentleness, and the idea that a mystery can be solved with patience and kindness rather than force — and that sharing what you have doesn't make you disappear, it makes you remembered. Isabella's own expression and posture should visibly evolve across the book, not just the world around her.
Illustration Philosophy
Each image should feel like a page from a gently mysterious children's storybook — quiet and atmospheric rather than frightening.
The illustration should naturally guide the viewer's attention:
First toward the central mystery or clue of the scene (the fading rose, the two trails, the animals, the crystal cave, the glowing jars).
Then toward Isabella actively investigating or responding.
Then toward the emotional expressions of curiosity and compassion.
Finally toward small environmental details that enrich the story.
Each slide will be paired with a short caption below the image (not rendered inside the illustration itself), inviting the reader to help Isabella solve that step's puzzle. Reserve approximately the lower 20–25% of every illustration as visually calm space (grass, forest floor, mist, or sky with minimal detail) so captions remain readable without covering important artwork.
Universal Illustration Rules
Camera distance: Keep a consistent medium-wide framing across every scene — far enough to show the atmospheric, fading environment, close enough to read Isabella's expression clearly.
One focal point per page: Every illustration should have a single clear visual anchor (the glowing rose, the two trails, the huddled animals, the crystal-covered cave mouth, the glowing jars, the returning color). Supporting details should stay secondary and never compete with it.
Limit scene complexity: No more than three distinct visual groupings per illustration (for example: Isabella, the central clue or mystery object, and the fading background). Avoid cluttering a single scene with too many competing details.
Camera exception: Step 5 (meeting the Color Keeper) shifts to a medium close-up, framing Isabella and the small creature intimately as the first color is shared, to make the emotional turning point feel personal rather than distant.
Environment Reference Sheet

The story's setting is a once-colorful forest that has faded almost entirely to soft gray, remaining visually consistent throughout the story except for the story's deliberate, tracked color reveals (see Color Progression System below).

The cave especially should read as a home, not a lair — evidence of a lonely creature living quietly, not a villain's hideout.

Art Style
Soft children's-storybook watercolor illustration, with one deliberate exception: this story uses a controlled, evolving color palette as its central visual device (see Color Progression System).
Style characteristics: loose hand-painted ink linework, visible brush texture, soft atmospheric shading, gentle mist and fading edges.
Characters: rounded simplified faces, small dot eyes, minimal facial features, soft body proportions, warm pink cheek blush (Isabella only — see Color Progression System).
Line style: dark brown or charcoal outlines (never pure black).
Backgrounds: loosely painted, atmospheric, impressionistic, fading softly toward edges with white paper negative space around the frame (not a full-bleed digital background).
Movement: Show motion only through small environmental movement — drifting gray leaves, gentle mist, a soft shimmer of light near the jars. Avoid exaggerated cartoon poses or action-comic effects.
Color Palette — Color Progression System
This story's central visual device is a world losing (and regaining) its color, so the palette must be tracked and controlled scene by scene rather than applied uniformly.
The rule: The environment, animals, and all objects are rendered in soft, muted grayscale watercolor (gentle charcoal, dove grey, warm parchment) unless a specific color has been introduced by the story at that point — in which case only that color appears, glowing gently against the gray. Isabella herself is the one exception throughout the entire book — she always appears in her own natural, warm coloring (olive-brown skin, dark wavy hair, and a soft rust-orange explorer's outfit), since she is untouched by whatever has drained the world's color. This makes her a visual anchor of warmth and hope in every scene.
Color reveal tracking, scene by scene:
Title Slide: Fully grayscale world. Only Isabella carries any color.
Step 1: The rose glows red — the story's first and only color besides Isabella. As it fades, Isabella keeps one final red petal, which travels with her (tucked into her braid, just above her ear) as a small, quiet reminder of color through the rest of the book.
Step 2: Still grayscale except Isabella and her kept red petal.
Step 3: Still grayscale except Isabella and the red petal. The animals remain fully gray, dulled and fading.
Step 4: Still mostly grayscale, but soft, flickering multi-color light (rainbow pulses — pale blue, gold, green) glows faintly between the cracks of the crystal-covered cave entrance, hinting at what's inside without fully revealing it.
Step 5: The cave interior reveals many colors at once — each glass jar glows with its own distinct stored color (orange, green, blue, and others). When Isabella opens the first jar, a bright blue swirls free into the air. This is the first color released back into the world.
Final Victory Slide: Full, vivid color floods the entire scene — every flower, animal, and leaf regains its natural color, and the sky brightens. This is the only scene in the book rendered in complete, unrestricted color, with one deliberate exception: the Color Keeper itself remains mostly soft gray, with only the subtlest warm highlights. This is intentional, not an error — it reinforces the story's theme that the creature never needed to change who it was to be accepted.
Avoid ever letting a color "leak" into a scene before the story has revealed it — this rule is essential to the book's visual storytelling and should never be broken for the sake of a prettier illustration.

speech bubbles
comic panels
game UI
interface buttons

heavy black outlines

true black-and-white or high-contrast photographic grayscale — the "gray world" should always read as soft, muted watercolor with warm undertones (warm grays, soft charcoal, and parchment tones), never cold or stark, and never a literal monochrome photo

Character Reference Sheet
Isabella: 10 years old, Hispanic, warm olive-brown skin, thick dark wavy hair in a loose braid, warm brown eyes, a soft rust-orange explorer's vest over a cream shirt, brown trousers, sturdy boots, a small satchel across her shoulder. Personality: Curious, gentle, patient, and empathetic — she solves the mystery by listening and understanding, not by force. She is the only character who always appears in full natural color.
The Color Keeper: a small, gentle creature about the size of a large house cat, with a soft pear-shaped body and a simple, rounded silhouette free of any sharp edges. Its pale gray skin has the delicate texture of handmade watercolor paper or velvety moss. It has a large, rounded head that blends smoothly into its body with no visible neck, oversized round eyes full of curiosity and loneliness, a tiny button nose, and a small, expressive mouth. Short, rounded arms with three stubby fingers gently hug its jars of color, with little oval feet and tiny leaf-shaped ear flaps completing its soft, forest-rooted appearance. Personality: Shy, lonely, and remorseful rather than villainous — it took the colors out of fear of being forgotten, not out of cruelty. It should always be illustrated as sympathetic and small, never threatening.
The Color Keeper's Posture Arc: its physical design never changes across the book — only its posture does. Through Steps 4 and most of Step 5, it stays curled up protectively, shoulders tucked, eyes timid and shy. As Step 5 resolves, it slowly begins standing a little taller and relaxing its shoulders, smiling for the first time. Crucially, even after the colors return to the world in the Final Slide, the Color Keeper remains mostly soft gray, with only the subtlest warm highlights — it is the one deliberate exception to the Final Slide's full-color rule (see Color Progression System below). It never needed to change who it was to be accepted and remembered; only its posture and expression change, never its coloring.
The Fox, Rabbit, and Owl: minor woodland animals appearing only in Step 3, all rendered in soft, dulled grayscale like the rest of the fading forest. The fox is the one who speaks; the rabbit and owl huddle close beside it, watching quietly. Their natural colors, hidden until the Final Slide, should be defined now so their grayscale tones carry the correct underlying values: the fox is rust-red with a cream underbelly, the rabbit is warm brown with soft tan patches, and the owl is speckled tawny-brown with cream flecking. In Step 3, render each in the grayscale tone that would naturally correspond to these colors (e.g., the fox a slightly darker charcoal-grey than the rabbit's lighter dove-grey) so their reveal in the Final Slide feels like an accurate continuation, not a redesign.
Character Consistency Rules Isabella's height, hairstyle, clothing colors, and facial proportions should remain identical throughout every illustration, and she must always appear in her full natural coloring even as the world around her stays gray. The Color Keeper's size, shape, and shy posture should remain consistent between Steps 4 and 5 and the Final Slide. Countdown to Liftoff — Illustration Guide
Story Overview
Four best friends — Aiden, Zara, Milo, and Nadia — have signed up for the Junior Science Rocket Competition, where teams build and launch a real model rocket for a chance to win the Golden Nozzle Trophy. There's just one problem: they have no money, no plan, and no rocket. With only a few weeks until launch day, the team will need help at every step — raising funds, cracking the math, gathering parts, fixing a shaky test flight, and clearing one last obstacle before the big day.
The story progresses through: Raising Funds → Finding the Formula → Gathering Parts → Fixing the Wobble → Weathering the Storm → Liftoff
The emotional progression: Determination → Discovery → Teamwork → Problem-Solving → Resolve → Pride
Each illustration should communicate resourcefulness, teamwork, and the idea that real engineering is a process of small fixes and careful iteration, not a single stroke of genius.
Illustration Philosophy
Each image should feel like a page from a bright, energetic children's storybook about invention and teamwork.
The illustration should naturally guide the viewer's attention:
First toward the main problem or task of the scene (the empty jar, the missing book, the packed shelves, the wobbly rocket, the storm clouds).
Then toward the four kids actively working together to solve it.
Then toward the emotional expressions of determination and teamwork.
Finally toward small environmental details that enrich the story.
Each slide will be paired with a short caption below the image (not rendered inside the illustration itself), phrased as a reader action ("Help the kids..."). Reserve approximately the lower 20–25% of every illustration as visually calm space (sky, ground, or workbench surface with minimal detail) so captions remain readable without covering important artwork.
Universal Illustration Rules
Camera distance: Keep a consistent medium-wide framing across every scene — far enough to show the workshop or environment context, close enough to read each kid's expression clearly.
One focal point per page: Every illustration should have a single clear visual anchor (the lemonade stand, the library shelf, the parts checklist, the rocket being fixed, the storm clearing, the launch itself). Supporting details should stay secondary and never compete with it.
Limit scene complexity: No more than three distinct visual groupings per illustration (for example: the kids, the central task or object, and the background environment). Avoid cluttering a single scene with too many competing activities.
Camera exception: Challenge 4 (fixing the wobbly rocket) shifts to a medium close-up on the rocket's nose cone, fins, and engine, making the diagnostic, hands-on fix feel precise and satisfying.

The Mission to Harmony Station — Illustration Guide
Story Overview
Ten-year-old Do-yoon, a young astronaut from Korea, has just graduated from the Junior Space Academy. His very first mission is to deliver a glowing Energy Crystal to Harmony Station, a peaceful space station where scientists, engineers, friendly robots, and families live and work together. Without the crystal, the station's power is fading — its lights are dim, its communication system is offline, and its greenhouse can no longer grow fresh food. Although everyone believes Do-yoon is ready, he quietly wonders if he's good enough for such an important mission. As he travels through space, each challenge teaches him that great astronauts don't succeed because they never make mistakes — they succeed because they stay calm, solve one problem at a time, and never stop learning.
The story progresses through: Preparing for Launch → Calibrating the Navigation System → Crossing the Asteroid Field → Repairing the Solar Panel → Docking at Harmony Station → Harmony Restored
The emotional progression: Self-Doubt → Preparation → Confidence → Problem-Solving → Leadership → Pride
Each illustration should communicate that great astronauts aren't fearless — they're calm, prepared, and willing to solve one problem at a time. Do-yoon's growing confidence should be visible entirely through his posture and how he handles his equipment, never through narration alone.
Illustration Philosophy
Each image should feel like a page from a warm, gently futuristic children's storybook — more cozy sci-fi picture book than hard science fiction.
The illustration should naturally guide the viewer's attention:
First toward the central problem or task of the scene (missing equipment, a lost star map, the asteroid field, the stuck solar panel, the docking approach).
Then toward Do-yoon actively solving it, equipment and posture in clear view.
Then toward his growing calm and confidence.
Finally toward small environmental details that enrich the story.
Each slide will be paired with a short caption below the image (not rendered inside the illustration itself), phrased as a reader action ("Help Do-yoon..."). Reserve approximately the lower 20–25% of every illustration as visually calm space (starfield, console surface, or station floor with minimal detail) so captions remain readable without covering important artwork.
Universal Illustration Rules
Camera distance: Keep a consistent medium-wide framing across every scene — far enough to show the spacecraft or station context, close enough to read Do-yoon's expression and posture clearly.
One focal point per page: Every illustration should have a single clear visual anchor (the missing gear, the star map, the safe path through the asteroids, the stuck panel, the docking port). Supporting details should stay secondary and never compete with it.
Limit scene complexity: No more than three distinct visual groupings per illustration (for example: Do-yoon, the central task or object, and the background environment). Avoid cluttering a single scene with too many competing details.
Camera exception (wide): Challenge 4 (the spacewalk) shifts to a wider, more awe-filled framing than usual — pulling back enough to show Do-yoon small against the vastness of space and the spacecraft, emphasizing scale and quiet courage.
Camera exception (close): Challenge 5 (docking and delivering the crystal) shifts to a medium close-up on the docking controls and the glowing crystal, making the story's most precise, important moment feel intimate.
Design register: Keep all spacecraft, tools, and station architecture rounded, friendly, and gently futuristic — closer to a cozy picture-book spaceship than realistic hard-science hardware.

The Lantern Through the Fog — Illustration Guide
Story Overview
A thick blanket of fog rolls over a little seaside village, and the fishing boats that sailed out early that morning can no longer find their way home. As the sun begins to set, worried families gather on the beach, watching the water. The old lighthouse on the hill has been dark for years — but Emma remembers her grandfather's words: "If the lighthouse ever shines again, every sailor will know the way home." She also remembers the secret path he once showed her. The trail is steep, the fog is thick, and Emma is afraid of the dark — but she picks up an old lantern anyway, and climbs.
The story progresses through:
Finding the Forgotten Path → Following the Markers → Gathering Supplies → Climbing the Tower → Lighting the Beacon → Coming Home
The emotional progression:
Determination → Focus → Urgency → Quiet Courage → Triumph → Belonging
Each illustration should communicate that courage isn't the absence of fear — it's carrying a small light forward anyway. Emma's fear of the dark should never be stated through narration alone; it should be visible in how she holds her lantern in every single scene.

Illustration Philosophy
Each image should feel like a page from a warm, atmospheric children's storybook — foggy and hushed, but never frightening.
The illustration should naturally guide the viewer's attention:

First toward the central obstacle or task of the scene (the hidden trail, a glowing marker, the empty oil lamp, the spiral stairs, the unlit beacon).
Then toward Emma actively responding to it, lantern always in view.
Then toward the quiet emotional shift in her posture and grip on the lantern.
Finally toward small environmental details that enrich the story.
Each slide will be paired with a short caption below the image (not rendered inside the illustration itself), drawn from the story's "After Completing the Level" lines. Reserve approximately the lower 20–25% of every illustration as visually calm space (fog, grass, stone, or sky with minimal detail) so captions remain readable without covering important artwork.

Universal Illustration Rules
Camera distance: Keep a consistent medium-wide framing across every scene — far enough to show the foggy, atmospheric environment, close enough to read Emma's expression and grip on the lantern clearly.
One focal point per page: Every illustration should have a single clear visual anchor (the hidden trailhead, a glowing seashell marker, the supply shed, the spiral stairs, the lit beacon, the returning boats). Supporting details should stay secondary and never compete with it.
Limit scene complexity: No more than three distinct visual groupings per illustration (for example: Emma, the central obstacle, and the foggy background). Avoid cluttering a single scene with too many competing details.
Camera exception: Step 5 (lighting the beacon) shifts to a medium close-up on Emma's hands, the lamp, and the match, making the "quiet moment before it happens" feel intimate and suspenseful.
The lantern is the emotional instrument of the story. How Emma holds it — gripped tightly in both hands, held out like a shield, set down to free her hands, held loosely at her side — should visibly track her fear turning into quiet courage across the book. See Body Language Progression below.
